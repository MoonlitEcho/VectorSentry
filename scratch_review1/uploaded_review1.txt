Production vector databases silently degrade in retrieval quality when a subset of documents is re-embedded by a different generation of the embedding model than the rest of the corpus — after a provider update, a model migration, or an incomplete backfill.

### Notes:
The core problem: a vector database can end up with documents embedded by two different generations of a model — say, half by an old provider version, half by a new one — after an update. Cosine similarity across those two generations is meaningless, but nothing errors out. It just quietly retrieves worse results. This is Review I, so we haven't run the full benchmark yet — that's planned Phase II work. What we're presenting today is the mechanism, the industry evidence it really happens, and exactly how we intend to quantify it: a controlled benchmark mixing two real open embedding models over a real IR corpus, measuring nDCG@10 as contamination rises. And this happens in the real world — OpenAI and Cohere have both shipped embedding updates that silently changed their models' internal geometry. Likely follow-up: 'why haven't you run this yet?' — answer: this review is a one-year project's starting pitch; the benchmark, detector, and scheduler are all Phase II build-and-evaluate work, scoped deliberately so it's achievable well within the year.

PROPOSED CONCEPT
Proposed Concept — VectorSentry

Prioritize contamination remediation the way an incident-response team would — not by fixing everything, and not by fixing arbitrarily, but by ranking WHAT to fix first under a real budget, using real traffic and real severity signals, verified safe before every change goes live.
For every flagged document, the scheduler chooses exactly one of three actions:

Full Re-embed
Patch
Defer
High cost   •   Full quality recovery
Low cost   •   Partial recovery
Zero cost   •   Zero recovery
Re-run the document through the current embedding model — the ground-truth fix.
A lightweight learned Procrustes/linear transform (Drift-Adapter-style) realigns the old embedding — cheap, imperfect.
Leave it contaminated for now — the mathematically correct choice when severity × traffic is low relative to cost.

### Notes:
The concept, in one sentence: treat contamination remediation as an incident-response prioritization problem, not a batch job. Concretely, for every document our detector flags, VectorSentry's scheduler picks exactly one of three actions — full re-embed for the documents that matter most, a cheap patch for the ones where partial recovery is good enough, or defer for the ones not worth spending budget on at all. That third option, Defer, is deliberately part of the formal action set, not an afterthought — sometimes the mathematically correct answer really is 'not yet,' and pretending otherwise is how uniform strategies waste budget.

### Notes:
This is the proposed architecture — the pipeline we plan to build. A live vector database feeds into Detect, which will score documents against a threshold calibrated from a known-clean baseline — not an oracle, exactly what a real operator would have. Prioritize is our scheduler design — the knapsack solver deciding full re-embed, patch, or defer for every flagged document. Migrate builds the proposed fix as a shadow copy — the live index is never touched directly, matching real zero-downtime deployment practice. Verify is the safety gate: it will check the shadow against a curated set of high-value 'golden queries' and only allow cutover if nothing regresses; on failure, it rolls back automatically and either retries with more budget or defers, so production is never silently degraded. The whole loop is designed to run autonomously and continuously — 'Live Sentry' is the proof-of-concept module we plan to build to demonstrate that, polling every two to five seconds with zero manual intervention beyond the initial trigger.

### Notes:
Our methodology follows standard software-engineering modular decomposition — four modules, each with a strict input/output contract, so each can be unit-tested and even swapped independently. Detection and Migration/Verification are adapted from established practice — KNN-OOD from Sun et al. 2022, shadow-build-and-cutover from real zero-downtime deployment patterns. Prioritization, Module 2, is our own contribution — the knapsack formulation grounded in Sinha and Zoltners' 1979 Operations Research paper, applied here for the first time to embedding remediation. We deliberately chose this framework — a well-understood exact optimization technique — over something like reinforcement learning, because it's provably optimal at small scale, has a fast well-understood approximation for production scale, and every decision it makes is explainable, which matters a great deal for a system that's allowed to modify a production database.

NOVELTY & CREATIVITY
What's Genuinely New Here

First application (to our literature) of a Multiple-Choice Knapsack formulation to embedding-contamination remediation — prior OR knapsack work targets resource allocation, not vector-DB integrity.
A formal 3-way action space {Full Re-embed, Patch, Defer} where every reviewed prior work offers only a binary or single-fix choice.
A closed detect ? prioritize ? migrate ? verify loop with an automated safety gate — prior work stops at detection or at a one-shot patch, with no verified-safe deployment step.

Novelty

Reframing 'which documents to fix' as a classic combinatorial-optimization problem (knapsack) rather than a heuristic ranking rule — brings 45 years of provably-optimal OR theory to a brand-new problem domain.
Traffic-weighting severity: a rarely-queried contaminated document and a heavily-queried one are NOT equally urgent — this benefit function borrows directly from Zipf-distributed real-world query traffic (Breslau et al., 1999).
The Defer action is a first-class, mathematically justified choice — not a fallback — deliberately spending zero budget when severity x traffic doesn't clear the cost bar.

Creativity

### Notes:
On novelty: to the fifteen papers we reviewed, nobody has applied a knapsack formulation to vector-database contamination specifically — knapsack optimization is well-established in Operations Research generally, but not in this domain. Nobody offers a three-way action choice either — it's always 'detect it' or 'here's one fix.' And nobody closes the loop with an automated, verified-safe deployment gate. On creativity — the traffic-weighting idea specifically came from recognizing that real query logs are Zipf-distributed, a fact established in 1999 networking literature, and realizing that fact should directly shape which documents get budget first. That's borrowing an insight from a completely different subfield — caching and CDN traffic — and applying it here.
