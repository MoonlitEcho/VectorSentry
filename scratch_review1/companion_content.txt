4. Viva Preparation — Question Bank
Grouped by theme. Answers are written to be spoken concisely (15–45 seconds each) — practice them out loud, don't read them verbatim in the review.
Problem & Motivation
Q: Why does mixed-generation contamination happen in practice, and why doesn't the system just error out?
A: Because cosine similarity between two different embedding-model generations is mathematically well-defined — it always returns a number between -1 and 1 — it's just not meaningful. The index has no way to know the geometry changed, so it keeps returning results with quietly worse relevance, never an exception.
Q: How did you measure the 27.5% quality drop without a real production system?
A: We built a controlled, seeded benchmark: 5,000 real documents from BEIR SciFact, embedded once with each of two real open sentence-transformer models (Gen A and Gen B), then mixed at controlled contamination ratios. It's synthetic in the sense that we control the ratio, but every embedding and every query is real.
Q: Isn't this just a rare edge case?
A: No — Pan et al.'s 2024 VLDB Journal survey counts 20+ commercial vector-database systems now deployed, and every major embedding provider (OpenAI, Cohere, and the open sentence-transformers ecosystem) ships model updates multiple times a year. Any team that can't afford to re-embed its entire corpus on every update hits this.
Methodology & Design Choices
Q: Why formulate this as a knapsack problem instead of a simpler heuristic (e.g. just sort by severity)?
A: A pure severity sort ignores that different documents have very different costs (full re-embed vs. patch vs. defer) and very different query traffic. Knapsack is the correct formulation because it's specifically designed for 'maximize value under one shared budget, with mutually-exclusive choices per item' — which is exactly our structure. A heuristic sort can't guarantee any optimality bound; our frontier-greedy is within 0.14% of the true optimum on a stress test where naive sorting was off by 88%.
Q: Why not use reinforcement learning or a learned policy instead?
A: Explainability and safety. Every decision our scheduler makes can be traced to a specific benefit/cost calculation, which matters enormously for a system allowed to modify a production database. An RL policy is a black box we'd have far less confidence deploying without extensive additional validation we didn't have time for in Phase I.
Q: What exactly does 'Defer' mean, and why is it a real action rather than just 'do nothing'?
A: Defer is a formal, budget-zero action chosen by the optimizer when a document's severity × traffic doesn't justify its fix cost relative to better uses of the budget. It's mathematically identical to any other action in the knapsack — the optimizer can prove it's the right choice, not just default to it.
Q: How does the safety gate actually decide pass/fail?
A: It re-evaluates a curated set of 'golden queries' — high-value, known-important queries — against the shadow-built candidate index. If retrieval quality (nDCG) on that golden set would regress beyond a tolerance threshold, the gate aborts the cutover and either retries with more budget or defers the affected documents.
Results & Evaluation
Q: Your scheduler loses in 10 of 30 test combinations — isn't that a weakness?
A: It's a disclosed trade-off, not a hidden weakness. All 10 losses are exactly the cases where our own safety gate aborted a plan to protect production quality — the same gate that makes the system safe to deploy at all. A system willing to sometimes recover less quality in exchange for a hard guarantee against silent regressions is, we'd argue, the correct choice for live infrastructure.
Q: How do you know your 0.14% optimality gap generalizes beyond the stress test?
A: We validated it two ways: the primary configuration degenerates to a near-uniform-cost knapsack where frontier-greedy and exact ILP both converge to ~0% gap trivially; the stress-test configuration was deliberately tuned to create a genuinely non-trivial three-way cost/benefit trade-off, and frontier-greedy still tracked the exact solver to within 0.14% there. Both regimes point the same direction.
Q: Why BEIR SciFact specifically, and only 5,000 documents?
A: BEIR SciFact is a real, standard, peer-reviewed IR benchmark with real relevance judgments, which lets us compute real nDCG/recall rather than a proxy metric. 5,000 documents keeps the exact-ILP baseline solvable in reasonable time for comparison — production-scale validation (millions of documents) is explicitly named as future work, not claimed as already done.
Novelty, Scope & Future Work
Q: What stops someone from saying 'this is just Drift-Adapter with extra steps'?
A: Drift-Adapter (and every other patching paper we reviewed) applies its fix uniformly to the whole corpus — it has no notion of a budget or of choosing per-document. Our contribution is the decision layer above that: which documents get which action under a shared budget, which is a distinct Operations-Research problem (Sinha & Zoltners, 1979) that none of our reviewed patching papers address.
Q: What would you do differently with more time?
A: Three things, honestly: validate at real production scale (millions of documents, not 5,000), integrate against a real vector-DB API instead of our own benchmark harness, and get an independent second corpus to corroborate the headline quality-drop numbers rather than relying on BEIR SciFact alone.
Q: Is the knapsack solver going to scale to millions of documents?
A: The exact ILP solver won't — that's why frontier-greedy exists, and it's near-linear time. We validated it stays within 0.14% of optimal at the scale we tested; extending that validation to millions of documents is exactly the kind of scale-up question we've scoped as Phase II work rather than claiming it's already proven.

Problem addressed
How to detect out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs in deep-learning feature spaces without assuming a parametric (e.g. Gaussian) distribution over in-distribution features.
Methodology / technique
Non-parametric k-nearest-neighbor (KNN) distance in the penultimate-layer embedding space; an input is flagged OOD if its distance to its k-th nearest in-distribution neighbor exceeds a calibrated threshold.
Dataset / tools
ImageNet-1k and CIFAR benchmark suites; standard OOD-detection evaluation protocol (FPR95, AUROC).
Results / key findings
KNN-based scoring matches or exceeds parametric OOD detectors while making no distributional assumptions — directly transferable to any embedding space, including text/document embeddings.
Strengths & limitations
Model-agnostic, no retraining required, strong empirical performance. Requires storing (or indexing) the full in-distribution reference set; sensitive to choice of k; does not address what action to take once an OOD point is flagged.
Relation to this project
This is the detection mechanism VectorSentry's Module 1 (Detection) adapts — our KNN-OOD scorer is a direct application of this method to document embeddings, calibrated against a clean-baseline percentile rather than a labeled OOD set.
Problem addressed
The OOD-detection literature had grown fragmented across vision, NLP, and general ML; no unified taxonomy connected detection method to deployment scenario.
Methodology / technique
Systematic literature survey and taxonomy of OOD-detection approaches, organized by detection paradigm (density-based, distance-based, classifier-based) and deployment context.
Dataset / tools
Meta-analysis across the surveyed literature; no single benchmark of its own.
Results / key findings
Confirms distance/density-based methods (including KNN-OOD) as a mature, well-validated family, but finds domain-specific OOD problems — including embedding-generation drift in vector search — remain largely unaddressed by existing taxonomy.
Strengths & limitations
Comprehensive, recent (2024), gives confidence that our detection choice (KNN-OOD) sits on a mature methodological foundation. A survey, not a novel method; provides no new empirical results of its own.
Relation to this project
Confirms Cluster A of our literature landscape is mature and validates KNN-OOD as a defensible, well-grounded detector choice rather than an ad hoc one.
Problem addressed
Existing anomaly-detection methods (distance- or density-based) scale poorly and implicitly assume anomalies are far from normal points in a specific geometric sense.
Methodology / technique
Isolates anomalies by randomly partitioning feature space; anomalies require fewer random splits to isolate than normal points, giving an anomaly score from average path length across an ensemble of random trees.
Dataset / tools
Standard anomaly-detection benchmarks (e.g. UCI-derived outlier datasets).
Results / key findings
Achieves linear time complexity and low memory, with detection performance competitive with distance-based methods, especially at scale.
Strengths & limitations
Highly scalable; no distance computation needed; well-established, widely implemented (scikit-learn). General-purpose; not evaluated on embedding-space anisotropy or high-dimensional dense-vector geometry specifically.
Relation to this project
Evaluated as an alternative Phase-1-baseline detector alongside KNN-OOD and LOF; included in our detection comparison (sub-phase 1C) even though KNN-OOD was ultimately the strongest performer on our benchmark.
Problem addressed
Global-distance anomaly definitions fail when normal-point density varies across a dataset — a point can be locally anomalous without being globally distant from all data.
Methodology / technique
Local Outlier Factor: compares a point's local density to the local density of its neighbors, giving a relative, density-aware anomaly score.
Dataset / tools
Synthetic and real-world clustering/outlier benchmarks used in the original SIGMOD evaluation.
Results / key findings
Detects anomalies that pure distance-based methods miss when data density is non-uniform — relevant because contaminated document embeddings do not form a uniform-density cluster.
Strengths & limitations
Foundational, still a standard anomaly-detection baseline 25 years later; principled handling of variable density. Same generality gap as Isolation Forest — general-purpose, not tuned for embedding-generation mismatch specifically; computationally heavier than KNN-OOD at scale.
Relation to this project
The third detector compared in our Phase 1 detection benchmark (sub-phase 1C); underperformed KNN-OOD on our primary configuration, documented honestly rather than omitted.
Problem addressed
Full re-embedding of a corpus after an embedding-model update is computationally expensive; systems need a cheap way to keep old embeddings usable after a model change.
Methodology / technique
A lightweight learned adapter (linear / orthogonal-Procrustes / small MLP variants) that maps old-generation embeddings into the new generation's space without querying the new model on the original documents.
Dataset / tools
Dense-retrieval benchmarks (BEIR-style); evaluated across multiple embedding-model version pairs.
Results / key findings
Recovers 95–99% of full re-embedding's retrieval recall at roughly 1% of the compute cost.
Strengths & limitations
Extremely cost-efficient; directly validates that cheap post-hoc realignment is viable, not just full re-embed. Applies one uniform adapter to the entire corpus — does not decide which documents most need the (still imperfect) fix under a constrained budget; single-author work, published 2025 (very recent, limited independent replication yet).
Relation to this project
This is the single most load-bearing paper for our own contribution: VectorSentry's Patch action is directly modeled on Drift-Adapter's approach, and our research gap is precisely the budget-aware, per-document decision layer this paper does not provide.
Problem addressed
Deploying a new, better embedding model normally requires re-encoding (backfilling) the entire existing corpus before it can be used safely alongside the new model's outputs.
Methodology / technique
Trains the new embedding model with an explicit backward-compatibility objective so its outputs are directly comparable to the old model's outputs, avoiding the need to re-embed old data at all.
Dataset / tools
Large-scale industrial retrieval/recommendation datasets.
Results / key findings
New models can be deployed without any backfill while preserving retrieval quality against the legacy embedding population.
Strengths & limitations
Solves the same underlying problem (mixed-generation embeddings) from the model-training side rather than the post-hoc-repair side — gives a valuable contrasting perspective. Requires control over the training of the new embedding model itself — not applicable to teams consuming third-party embedding APIs (OpenAI, Cohere) where the model is a black box; still a single uniform strategy, not per-document.
Relation to this project
Represents the 'prevent the problem at training time' alternative to our 'detect and remediate after the fact' approach — discussed in our literature landscape as a complementary, not competing, strategy, since it doesn't apply when the embedding provider is external.
Problem addressed
Aligning two independently-trained word-embedding spaces (e.g. across languages) without any parallel supervision data.
Methodology / technique
Adversarial training to obtain an initial rough mapping between spaces, refined via iterative orthogonal Procrustes alignment (MUSE).
Dataset / tools
fastText monolingual word embeddings across multiple languages; bilingual dictionary induction and word-translation evaluation.
Results / key findings
A single learned orthogonal linear transform can align two independently-trained embedding spaces to near-supervised accuracy, with no parallel training signal.
Strengths & limitations
Landmark, heavily-cited (2018) result establishing that geometric realignment between embedding spaces is a solved, well-understood sub-problem. Designed for cross-lingual word embeddings, which are more nearly isomorphic than two generations of a sentence-embedding model; assumes no budget constraint on applying the transform.
Relation to this project
Theoretical foundation for the orthogonal-Procrustes patch mechanism used in VectorSentry's Patch action — included as a foundational/landmark work predating our 2020–2024 recency window deliberately, to ground the technique historically.
Problem addressed
Exact nearest-neighbor search does not scale to large, high-dimensional vector collections; existing approximate methods traded off recall, speed, and memory poorly.
Methodology / technique
Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs — a multi-layer proximity graph enabling logarithmic-complexity approximate search.
Dataset / tools
SIFT1M, GIST1M, DEEP1B and related large-scale ANN benchmark datasets.
Results / key findings
HNSW achieves state-of-the-art recall/speed trade-offs and became the de facto standard indexing algorithm behind most production vector databases.
Strengths & limitations
Extremely well-validated; near-universal industrial adoption (FAISS, Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, Qdrant all implement HNSW or a close variant). Assumes a geometrically consistent embedding space across the whole index — an assumption silently violated by mixed-generation contamination, which is exactly the failure mode this project targets.
Relation to this project
Foundational systems literature: explains why contamination is dangerous specifically for HNSW-indexed production systems, and grounds our System Architecture slide's framing of 'the live index.'
Problem addressed
Vector database systems proliferated rapidly (2020–2024) with no unified survey of their architectures, indexing strategies, and operational trade-offs.
Methodology / technique
Systematic survey and taxonomy of 20+ commercial and open-source vector database management systems.
Dataset / tools
Comparative analysis across surveyed systems; no single experimental benchmark.
Results / key findings
Documents the rapid growth of the vector-database ecosystem (20+ systems) but identifies post-deployment embedding-integrity management as an open, under-addressed operational concern.
Strengths & limitations
Recent (2024), comprehensive, published in a top systems venue (VLDB Journal); gives strong quantitative evidence of real-world relevance. Surveys system architecture, not the specific contamination-remediation problem; provides no method of its own.
Relation to this project
Primary evidentiary source for our Real-World Relevance slide's '20+ commercial systems' statistic, and for establishing that this is an active, growing production domain.
Problem addressed
Text-embedding model evaluation was fragmented across inconsistent, task-specific benchmarks, making model comparison unreliable.
Methodology / technique
A unified benchmark spanning 8 embedding task types (retrieval, clustering, classification, STS, etc.) across 58+ datasets.
Dataset / tools
MTEB benchmark suite itself, aggregating many existing IR/NLP datasets.
Results / key findings
No single embedding model dominates across all task types — model choice is task-dependent, and models are frequently updated/replaced as better ones emerge on the leaderboard.
Strengths & limitations
Became the standard reference for embedding-model quality comparison; widely adopted (Hugging Face leaderboard). A static snapshot benchmark — does not evaluate post-deployment drift or mixed-generation scenarios, and does not test what happens when a corpus is only partially migrated to a new model.
Relation to this project
Explains the practical trigger for our problem: MTEB-driven model churn is exactly why production teams keep swapping embedding models, creating the mixed-generation scenario VectorSentry addresses.
Problem addressed
Prior work assumed embedding anisotropy (vectors clustering in a narrow cone rather than spreading isotropically) uniformly harms downstream dense-retrieval quality; this assumption had not been rigorously tested.
Methodology / technique
Empirical analysis measuring anisotropy across multiple sentence-embedding models and correlating it with actual retrieval performance.
Dataset / tools
Standard IR and semantic-textual-similarity (STS) benchmarks.
Results / key findings
Anisotropy's practical harm to retrieval quality is more nuanced and model-dependent than the prior consensus assumed.
Strengths & limitations
Empirically rigorous, challenges a widely-cited assumption with direct evidence. Focuses on anisotropy within a single embedding generation, not the mixed-generation, multi-geometry scenario this project studies.
Relation to this project
Directly informed our decision to run a dedicated anisotropy stress test (sub-phase 1C bonus finding, aniso_stress_bge) confirming that a highly anisotropic model pairing can invert or nullify detector signal — an original empirical contribution of our own that extends this paper's question into the mixed-generation setting.
Problem addressed
Classic resource-allocation problem: given groups of mutually-exclusive items (choose at most one per group), each with a cost and value, and one shared budget, find the value-maximizing selection.
Methodology / technique
Exact branch-and-bound / dynamic-programming formulation of the Multiple-Choice Knapsack Problem (MCKP), plus efficient approximation bounds.
Dataset / tools
Not applicable — a theoretical Operations Research paper with worked numerical examples, not an empirical ML paper.
Results / key findings
Establishes the exact and approximate solution theory for the one-choice-per-group knapsack structure that underlies any '3 mutually exclusive actions per item, 1 shared budget' decision problem.
Strengths & limitations
Foundational (1979), mathematically rigorous, still the standard reference formulation for this exact problem structure 45+ years later. Purely theoretical; never applied to embedding remediation, vector databases, or any ML-systems problem — that application is entirely our own.
Relation to this project
This is the mathematical core of VectorSentry's Prioritization module (M2): our {Full Re-embed, Patch, Defer} per-document decision under one shared budget is a direct instantiation of this 1979 MCKP formulation, solved both exactly (ILP) and via a frontier-greedy approximation.
Problem addressed
Understanding the statistical distribution of real-world web request traffic, to inform caching-system design.
Methodology / technique
Empirical measurement and statistical fitting of web-request traffic logs.
Dataset / tools
Real web-proxy traffic logs.
Results / key findings
Real request traffic to web objects follows a Zipf-like (highly skewed, power-law) distribution — a small fraction of objects receive most of the requests.
Strengths & limitations
Landmark networking-systems result (1999), extremely well-validated and still cited as the standard traffic model in systems research. About web-object request traffic specifically, not vector-database query traffic — the extension to IR query traffic is an assumption we make, not something this paper itself demonstrates.
Relation to this project
Directly grounds VectorSentry's traffic-weighted benefit function: because real query traffic is Zipf-distributed, a rarely-queried contaminated document and a heavily-queried one are not equally urgent to fix — our benchmark's query-traffic model is built on this result.
Problem addressed
Conventional monitoring (exceptions, crash logs, uptime checks) fails to catch a distinct category of ML production failure: the system keeps running and returning outputs, but those outputs have silently degraded in quality.
Methodology / technique
Taxonomy and case-study review of silent-failure modes across deployed ML systems.
Dataset / tools
Case studies drawn from production ML incident reports.
Results / key findings
Establishes 'silent failure' as a distinct, systematically under-monitored category of production ML risk, separate from crashes or latency regressions.
Strengths & limitations
Names and frames precisely the failure category VectorSentry addresses; recent (2023); directly supports the 'why is this silent and dangerous' framing on our Problem Statement slide. General MLOps treatment across ML systems broadly — does not specifically address vector databases, embeddings, or retrieval quality.
Relation to this project
Provides the general MLOps vocabulary and framing (“silent failure”) that our Problem Statement slide applies specifically to mixed-generation embedding contamination.
Problem addressed
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems can produce unfaithful answers when retrieved context conflicts with the underlying language model's own internal knowledge; the relative contribution of retrieval quality to this failure was not well quantified.
Methodology / technique
Controlled empirical study varying retrieved-context correctness and measuring how often the LLM follows the (possibly wrong) retrieved context versus its own prior.
Dataset / tools
Question-answering datasets with deliberately perturbed / conflicting retrieved context.
Results / key findings
Retrieval quality is a first-order determinant of RAG faithfulness — degraded retrieval measurably degrades downstream answer correctness, not just relevance ranking.
Strengths & limitations
Directly connects retrieval-quality degradation (exactly what our contamination causes) to a concrete, user-visible downstream harm in RAG applications. ⚠ arXiv preprint at time of citation, not yet published in a peer-reviewed venue — flagged explicitly in the Literature-Guideline Compliance Audit below as a disclosed deviation from the “scholarly articles only” requirement, not a hidden one.
Relation to this project
Motivates why contamination matters beyond raw nDCG@10: in a RAG deployment, VectorSentry's problem translates directly into unfaithful, hallucination-prone generated answers.

Drift-Adapter: A lightweight framework for continuous dense retrieval adaptation
A lightweight learned adapter (linear / orthogonal-Procrustes / small MLP variants) that maps old-generation embeddings into the new generation's space without querying the new model on the original documents.
Dense-retrieval benchmarks (BEIR-style); evaluated across multiple embedding-model version pairs.
Recovers 95–99% of full re-embedding's retrieval recall at roughly 1% of the compute cost.
Applies one uniform adapter to the entire corpus — does not decide which documents most need the (still imperfect) fix under a constrained budget
6
Hu, Y., Bansal, S., Cao, K., Rao, R., Subbian, K., & Leskovec, J., 2022
Learning backward compatible embeddings
Trains the new embedding model with an explicit backward-compatibility objective so its outputs are directly comparable to the old model's outputs, avoiding the need to re-embed old data at all.
Large-scale industrial retrieval/recommendation datasets.
New models can be deployed without any backfill while preserving retrieval quality against the legacy embedding population.
Requires control over the training of the new embedding model itself — not applicable to teams consuming third-party embedding APIs (OpenAI, Cohere) where the model is a black box
7
Lample, G., Conneau, A., Ranzato, M., Denoyer, L., & Jégou, H., 2018
Word translation without parallel data
Adversarial training to obtain an initial rough mapping between spaces, refined via iterative orthogonal Procrustes alignment (MUSE).
fastText monolingual word embeddings across multiple languages; bilingual dictionary induction and word-translation evaluation.
A single learned orthogonal linear transform can align two independently-trained embedding spaces to near-supervised accuracy, with no parallel training signal.
Designed for cross-lingual word embeddings, which are more nearly isomorphic than two generations of a sentence-embedding model
8
Malkov, Y. A., & Yashunin, D. A., 2020
Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using hierarchical navigable small world graphs
Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs — a multi-layer proximity graph enabling logarithmic-complexity approximate search.
SIFT1M, GIST1M, DEEP1B and related large-scale ANN benchmark datasets.
HNSW achieves state-of-the-art recall/speed trade-offs and became the de facto standard indexing algorithm behind most production vector databases.
Assumes a geometrically consistent embedding space across the whole index — an assumption silently violated by mixed-generation contamination, which is exactly the failure mode this project targets.
9
Pan, J. J., Wang, J., & Li, G., 2024
Survey of vector database management systems
Systematic survey and taxonomy of 20+ commercial and open-source vector database management systems.
Comparative analysis across surveyed systems; no single experimental benchmark.
Documents the rapid growth of the vector-database ecosystem (20+ systems) but identifies post-deployment embedding-integrity management as an open, under-addressed operational concern.
Surveys system architecture, not the specific contamination-remediation problem
10
Muennighoff, N., Tazi, N., Magne, L., & Reimers, N., 2023
MTEB: Massive text embedding benchmark
A unified benchmark spanning 8 embedding task types (retrieval, clustering, classification, STS, etc.) across 58+ datasets.
MTEB benchmark suite itself, aggregating many existing IR/NLP datasets.
No single embedding model dominates across all task types — model choice is task-dependent, and models are frequently updated/replaced as better ones emerge on the leaderboard.
A static snapshot benchmark — does not evaluate post-deployment drift or mixed-generation scenarios, and does not test what happens when a corpus is only partially migrated to a new model.
11
Fuster Baggetto, A., & Fresno, V., 2022
Is anisotropy truly harmful for dense retrieval? Findings of the Association for Computational Linguistics: EMNLP 2022
Empirical analysis measuring anisotropy across multiple sentence-embedding models and correlating it with actual retrieval performance.
Standard IR and semantic-textual-similarity (STS) benchmarks.
Anisotropy's practical harm to retrieval quality is more nuanced and model-dependent than the prior consensus assumed.
Focuses on anisotropy within a single embedding generation, not the mixed-generation, multi-geometry scenario this project studies.
12
Sinha, P., & Zoltners, A. A., 1979
The multiple-choice knapsack problem
Exact branch-and-bound / dynamic-programming formulation of the Multiple-Choice Knapsack Problem (MCKP), plus efficient approximation bounds.
Not applicable — a theoretical Operations Research paper with worked numerical examples, not an empirical ML paper.
Establishes the exact and approximate solution theory for the one-choice-per-group knapsack structure that underlies any '3 mutually exclusive actions per item, 1 shared budget' decision problem.
Purely theoretical
13
Breslau, L., Cao, P., Fan, L., Phillips, G., & Shenker, S., 1999
Web caching and Zipf-like distributions: Evidence and implications
Empirical measurement and statistical fitting of web-request traffic logs.
Real web-proxy traffic logs.
Real request traffic to web objects follows a Zipf-like (highly skewed, power-law) distribution — a small fraction of objects receive most of the requests.
About web-object request traffic specifically, not vector-database query traffic — the extension to IR query traffic is an assumption we make, not something this paper itself demonstrates.
14
Karval, R., & Singh, A., 2023
Silent failures in machine learning production systems
Taxonomy and case-study review of silent-failure modes across deployed ML systems.
Case studies drawn from production ML incident reports.
Establishes 'silent failure' as a distinct, systematically under-monitored category of production ML risk, separate from crashes or latency regressions.
General MLOps treatment across ML systems broadly — does not specifically address vector databases, embeddings, or retrieval quality.
15
Wu, K., Wu, E., & Zou, J., 2024
How faithful are RAG models? Quantifying the tug-of-war between RAG and LLMs' internal prior
Controlled empirical study varying retrieved-context correctness and measuring how often the LLM follows the (possibly wrong) retrieved context versus its own prior.
Question-answering datasets with deliberately perturbed / conflicting retrieved context.
Retrieval quality is a first-order determinant of RAG faithfulness — degraded retrieval measurably degrades downstream answer correctness, not just relevance ranking.
